Projects & Research

Gravitational Indications of Subduction on Venus

M.S. Thesis · Baylor University

While Venus lacks global plate tectonics, there has been evidence of localized subduction. The goal of this study is to observe and forward model the gravity gradients of subducted slabs on Earth and Venus. Our modeled results are consistent with subduction in at least three locations on Venus: Artemis Corona, Quetzalpetlatl Corona, and Astkhik Plateau.

We also identified a trend at terrestrial and Venusian subduction zones of a higher geothermal gradient on the overriding plate side of the trench and a lower geothermal gradient on the outboard side. The modeled subducting slabs on Earth and Venus are found to be negatively buoyant, though Venus slabs tended to be slightly less negatively buoyant.

We hope these methods will be repeated for smaller coronae once gravity data from the VERITAS mission has been recovered.

Fallen Meteorite Classification

Machine Learning Project

The Meteoritical Society collects data on meteorites that have fallen to Earth — over 45,000 records including classification, location, mass, and collection year. While fallen meteorite flux is largely uniform across the globe, the geographical and meteoritic class distributions of collected meteorites are not.

This study seeks to understand what variables control meteorite collection and predict what types are more likely to be found in different geographical regions. Features explored include population, distance from nearest population center, precipitation, topography, and distance from the ocean.

A strong human collection bias appears associated with ease of locating meteorites and recognition — notably in the United States, deserts, and Antarctica, where conditions favor preservation and dedicated search programs like ANSMET operate.

The classification model works best at identifying iron and chondrite meteorites, suggesting a strong collection bias toward those classes relative to expected random flux.

Terrain Roughness from 3D Models

B.S. Thesis · Brigham Young University

Remote sensing, especially Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) imaging, can reveal surface roughness on Earth and other planetary bodies. Various geological features are known to have unique small-scale topography identifiable by their roughness at the radar wavelength scale.

During the Europlanet 2018 campaign, we obtained overlapping handheld camera images to derive 3D spatial models at several sites in the Danakil desert of Northeastern Ethiopia — a particularly flat arid region ideal for validation studies.

Research showed that 3D elevation models from these flats represent a roughness different from what is observed in radar, depending on the instrument wavelength sensitivity. If these roughness calculations are relevant to geological processes on other planets, this work improves our understanding of features that might be present where remote sensing shows apparently flat terrain.
